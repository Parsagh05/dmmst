\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report collects every experiment run for the paper: two single-event benchmarks (METABRIC, SUPPORT), three
multi-event datasets (a simulator with known truth, hsa\_synthetic, EBMT), simulator sweeps over eight properties of
the data, an evaluation of within-patient event ordering against true event times, tuning of the loss penalties, a
continuous vs.\ quantile vs.\ discretised input comparison, three generations of experiments on coded visit
histories (\S4.2 of the paper) and the end-to-end language model (\S3). The transformer trained with the
piecewise-constant-hazard likelihood (\Lpch{}) is the best or tied-best of the models we ran on every benchmark
dataset, is the most accurate learned model at ordering events within a patient, and needs about 5\,000 patients
to beat Cox; it remains below the published SurvTRACE. The ranking penalties \Lrank{} and \Lmul{} never improve a
proper score, and the within-subject C-index is shown to be gameable. The coded-sequence claim (\S4.2) is not
supported by our experiments, and the end-to-end language model works but is clearly weaker.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Summary of findings}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary of findings}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The base model is strong.} Our transformer with \Lpch{} has the best or tied-best \Ctd{} and the
        best calibration among neural models we ran on METABRIC (0.675), SUPPORT (0.619), hsa\_synthetic (0.867), EBMT
        (0.545; all models 0.50--0.55) and the simulator (0.713), and matches the published PC-Hazard, DeepHit and Cox values (Section~\ref{sec:single}).
  \item \textbf{It remains below the published SurvTRACE}: 0.675 vs.\ 0.691 (METABRIC) and 0.619 vs.\ 0.640 (SUPPORT).
  \item \textbf{It orders events within a patient best among learned models}, measured against the simulator's true
        event times; DeepHit, a competing-risk model, ranks patients well but orders events badly
        (Section~\ref{sec:true}). This supports the paper's general multi-event formulation.
  \item \textbf{\Lrank{} and \Lmul{} do not help.} Untuned they lower calibration and true ordering; tuned on
        validation they become indistinguishable from \Lpch{} (Section~\ref{sec:tune}).
  \item \textbf{The within-subject C-index can be gamed.} \Lmul{} raises it above the oracle while every true-order
        score gets worse (Section~\ref{sec:true}).
  \item \textbf{When a neural model pays off.} Cox matches the oracle for linear risk and collapses for non-linear
        risk; from $\approx$5\,000 patients the transformer leads clearly; it scales to 8 events (Section~\ref{sec:sweeps}).
  \item \textbf{MMV} cuts the error of predicted event times on observed patients (SUPPORT $-21\,\%$, METABRIC
        $-22\,\%$) at a cost in calibration (Section~\ref{sec:single}).
  \item \textbf{Numeric encoding.} With correct preprocessing, continuous and quantile encodings are equal and both
        beat 10-bin discretisation on METABRIC; on SUPPORT all three are equal (Section~\ref{sec:enc}).
  \item \textbf{Coded sequences (\S4.2) are not supported.} Even when the outcome depends on order and trends that a
        bag-of-codes table cannot see, no sequence model --- including a larger model with \texttt{[CLS]} pooling
        and recency encoding --- beats the table (Section~\ref{sec:ehr}).
  \item \textbf{The end-to-end LLM (\S3)} works end to end but trails our transformer by 0.06--0.09 \Ctd{}
        (Section~\ref{sec:llm}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulator} (\texttt{sat/data/simulate.py}): Weibull proportional hazards per event with a risk that
        mixes shared and event-specific parts, optional non-linearity, unobserved frailty, per-event timescales
        (``order spread'') and calibrated censoring, in the competing, semi-competing or non-competing regime. Its
        true cumulative incidence functions (CIFs), verified against Monte Carlo, give an \emph{oracle} row.
  \item \textbf{EHR simulator} (\texttt{sat/data/simulate\_ehr.py}): five years of visits with diagnosis codes
        (hypertension, diabetes, CKD, COPD), medications, labs with values (eGFR, HbA1c, FEV$_1$), exacerbations and
        noise codes; four outcomes (CKD progression, diabetic complication, COPD hospitalisation, death). A
        ``temporal share'' controls how much risk depends on order, trends and recency rather than current levels.
        In v1 the trend information was recoverable from counts and last values; v2 removes that shortcut.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Models}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ours}: numeric-value-embedding transformer (\S2.2, Fig.~2 of the paper) $\to$ shared MLP $\to$
        piecewise-constant-hazard head. Loss variants: \Lpch{}; $+$\Lrank{} (across patients, Eq.~4); $+$\Lmul{} (within
        patient, Eq.~5); the paper recipe \Lpch{}$+$\Lrank{}$+$\Lmul{}; $+$MMV (UniSurv mean/variance loss with weight
        $\lambda_v$); ``full'' = all four.
  \item \textbf{Baselines}: cause-specific Cox PH; DeepHit, DSM and MENSA as published, sharing our transformer backbone
        and using their own heads and losses; the oracle on simulated data.
  \item \textbf{End-to-end LLM} (\S3): DistilGPT2 (pretrained) or a 4-layer GPT-2 trained from scratch that reads
        the record as text and answers one token per time interval (\texttt{N}, \texttt{C}, \texttt{E}$k$);
        per-event curves are derived from the token probabilities.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Protocol}
Seed $s$ sets both the initialisation and the train/validation/test split $s$ (60/10/30\,\%); single-event and
multi-event benchmarks use 5 seeds, sweeps and the LLM 3. Checkpoints are selected on validation \Ctd{} with early
stopping. Tables report mean $\pm$ sd over seeds. ``Paired'' comparisons use the same seed for both models and report
the mean difference, the number of seeds on which the difference is positive, and a paired $t$-test $p$-value
(with 5 seeds the smallest Wilcoxon $p$ is 0.0625, so it is not used). All runs were executed on Kaggle (T4 GPU) from
the public repository; hyper-parameters are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}, and the corrections made before the
reported runs in Appendix~\ref{app:fixes}.

\paragraph{Reading Table~\ref{tab:true}.} Measured against the true event times, our \Lpch{} model is the best learned
model in both regimes, ahead of MENSA, Cox and DeepHit. Adding \Lmul{} or using the paper recipe makes every true-order

general multi-event formulation.

\section{Simulator sweeps}\label{sec:sweeps}
%=====================================================================
Each figure varies one property of the simulated data around the base scenario (3 splits; error bars: sd). The
dashed grey line is the oracle. Panels: within-subject C, \Ctd{}, within-C gain over population KM, and (sweeps of
notebook 11 only) true-order Brier.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_nonlin.pdf}
\caption{Non-linearity of the risk (0 = linear, 1 = fully non-linear).}\label{fig:nonlin}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Figure~\ref{fig:nonlin}.} For a linear risk Cox equals the oracle (\Ctd{} 0.741) and our model loses little
(0.736). As the risk becomes non-linear Cox collapses (0.613) while ours stays at 0.709 (oracle 0.741). This is the
clearest argument for a neural model.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_n.pdf}
\caption{Training-set size (500 to 10\,000 subjects).}\label{fig:n}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Figure~\ref{fig:n}.} The gap of our model to the oracle shrinks from 0.058 ($n{=}500$) to 0.011
($n{=}10\,000$); Cox's gap stays 0.05--0.07. Below 5\,000 subjects the two are comparable (Cox ahead at $n=500$ and
2\,500, the transformer at 1\,000); from $\approx$5\,000 subjects the transformer leads clearly. Each $n$ is a different
random dataset, so the small-$n$ points are noisy.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_events.pdf}
\caption{Number of events $K$ (2 to 8).}\label{fig:events}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Figure~\ref{fig:events}.} Our model stays $\approx$0.02 below the oracle at every $K$, Cox 0.05--0.07 below:
the framework scales with the number of events. \Lmul{} and the paper recipe are worse than \Lpch{} on true-order
Brier at every $K$ (e.g.\ 0.226 and 0.230 vs.\ 0.213 at $K{=}4$).

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_order_spread.pdf}
\caption{Spread of the events' typical timing (0: the covariates decide the order; 2: nearly the same order for every patient).}\label{fig:spread}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Figure~\ref{fig:spread}.} When the order is the same for everybody (spread 2) every model reaches the
oracle's within-C ($\approx$0.89--0.90) and there is nothing patient-specific to learn. When the order is
patient-specific (spread 0) the oracle reaches 0.699, \Lpch{} 0.679 and \Lmul{} 0.663: \Lmul{} is lowest exactly where it
was meant to help.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_shared.pdf}
\caption{Share of the risk common to all events (``shared disease pathway'').}\label{fig:shared}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Figure~\ref{fig:shared}.} The more risk the events share, the less patient-specific ordering there is to
learn; at a share of 1 even the oracle gains nothing over population ordering. Our model tracks the oracle at every
level, and \Lmul{} is never above \Lpch{}.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_frailty.pdf}
\caption{Unobserved shared frailty (dependence that the covariates cannot explain).}\label{fig:frailty}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Figure~\ref{fig:frailty}.} Frailty lowers every model and the oracle together (\Lpch{} \Ctd{} 0.714 $\to$
0.642 at frailty variance 2); no loss variant is more robust than \Lpch{}.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/sweep_censor.pdf}

\paragraph{Reading Tables~\ref{tab:ehr12}--\ref{tab:ehrfinal} and Figure~\ref{fig:ehr}.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{v1}: sequence and bag tie because the trend information could be recovered from counts and last values
        (a linear model on the bag recovers 66--86\,\% of the true risk).
  \item \textbf{v2} removes this shortcut (the bag recovers only 12--41\,\% for three of the four outcomes), and the
        oracle's lead over the best learned model grows with the temporal share: 0.013 (level), 0.029 (mixed),
        0.044 (order). The information is present in the history.
  \item \textbf{No sequence model extracts it.} The small sequence model beats the bag only in the level-only cohort
        ($+0.010$, 5/5, $p=0.046$), where no temporal information exists, and ties it elsewhere ($-0.001$, $-0.003$,
        $p\ge0.09$).
  \item \textbf{The larger model is worse}, for both inputs and in all cohorts ($-0.015$ to $-0.033$ \Ctd{}, 0/5,
        $p\le0.04$), most likely overfitting $\approx$3\,000 training patients. Recency encoding adds nothing
        ($p\ge0.18$).
  \item Cox on the bag table is the best learned model on these cohorts, consistent with their near-linear risk in
        the bag features.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Conclusion}: under the conditions tested --- 5\,000 patients, up to 256 tokens, small and medium transformers ---
the \S4.2 claim that modelling the coded sequence improves prediction over a summary table is not supported.

%=====================================================================
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
%=====================================================================
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Real multi-event data}: only EBMT, whose four categorical covariates carry little signal (\Ctd{}
        $\approx$0.54 for every model); MIMIC requires credentialed access and the paper's CKD/COPD/diabetes cohorts
        were not available.
  \item \textbf{Comparison with SurvTRACE} uses published numbers on different splits; our model has no multi-task
        head.
  \item \textbf{Number of splits}: 5 for benchmarks, 3 for sweeps and the LLM; small effects ($<0.005$) are not
        resolvable.
  \item \textbf{MAE-margin is unreliable on EBMT} (values above 7\,000 days): with long follow-up and heavy
        censoring the Kaplan--Meier best guess extrapolates far beyond the data. MAE is reported only on METABRIC,
        SUPPORT and simulated data.
  \item \textbf{True-order scores} assume independent events given the covariates when converting marginal curves into
        $P(T_a<T_b)$; this is exact for the simulator's non-competing regime and an approximation for semi-competing data.
  \item \textbf{\S4.2 sequences} were tested only on simulated records with up to 256 tokens and $\approx$3\,000
        training patients; larger real cohorts or pre-trained clinical models may behave differently.
\end{itemize}
